Content Validation Lab

Content validation involves raters who are asked about correspondence between items and various constructs. One of these is the construct of interest while the others are related “orbiting” constructs. Anderson and Gerbing (1991) have judges sort items; Hinkin and Tracey (1999) have them rate every item against every definition; Colquitt et al. (2019) turned both into correspondence and distinctiveness indices with empirical norms.

IMPORTANT NOTE: These procedures only look at whether the items match the construct. They do not consider whether the entire construct domain is covered by the items. See Alley et al. (2026) for discussion of this issue.

Suppose that we are doing a content validation of a measure of work engagement and that we are using job involvement and intrinsic motivation as orbiting constructs.

These are the definitions raters see when they sort or rate the items. Constructs, definitions, items, and data are editable examples. Colquitt et al.’s norms come from tasks with exactly this layout: one focal definition and two orbiting definitions.

Sorting items into definitions

Each judge reads the three definitions and assigns every item to the one it fits best. For each item, count how many judges chose each definition. Two indices summarize the counts. The proportion of substantive agreement (psa) asks how often judges put the item where you intended. The coefficient of substantive validity (csv) asks how far the intended definition beat its strongest rival, so an item that splits judges between two definitions is penalized twice.

Item-level significance uses an exact binomial test of the target count (Howard & Melloy, 2016). Colquitt et al. called psa a correspondence index and csv a distinctiveness index, and normed both at the scale level.

psa = nc / N
csv = (nc − no) / N
nc
judges who assigned the item to its intended definition
no
highest count for any other definition
N
judges who sorted the item
psa runs 0 to 1; csv runs −1 to 1, and 0 means a tie with the strongest rival.

Rating every item against every definition

Each judge rates how well each item matches each definition (Colquitt et al. used a 7-point scale). Hinkin and Tracey analyzed the ratings with ANOVA: an item shows content adequacy when its mean on the intended definition is significantly higher than its means on the other definitions. Because the same judges rate all three definitions, this page uses a one-way repeated-measures ANOVA with a Greenhouse-Geisser correction, followed by paired one-sided contrasts of the intended definition against each orbiting one.

Colquitt et al. rescaled the same ratings into HTC (Hinkin-Tracey correspondence) and HTD (Hinkin-Tracey distinctiveness).

HTC = Mfocal / A
HTD = (Mfocal − Morbiting) / (A − 1)
Mfocal
mean rating of the item on its intended definition
Morbiting
mean rating on the two orbiting definitions, averaged
A
number of scale anchors
HTC runs from 1/A to 1. HTD is a difference, so its typical values are much smaller than HTC’s; compare each index only with its own norms.

Populations and samples

The three Population means columns are yours to change. Each one is the mean rating an item would receive on that definition if every possible judge rated it. Raise an item’s focal mean, or push an orbiting mean up toward it, and see what happens.

Everything to the right of those columns comes from one sample of judges drawn from those populations. Each simulated judge has a personal leniency, adds random error, and rounds every rating to a whole scale anchor. The small numbers under each profile bar are that sample’s means. HTC, HTD, F, and the p values are all computed from the sample data, so they will not exactly match what the population means imply.

Click Draw new judges to replace the panel with a fresh sample from the same populations. Watch how far the statistics jump between draws: with 20 judges they move noticeably, and with 200 or more they settle close to the population values. A real pretest gives you only one draw.

What the F test tells you

F tests the null hypothesis that an item’s population mean ratings are equal on all three definitions (focal = orbiting 1 = orbiting 2). Each judge rates the item on every definition. That is what makes this a repeated-measures ANOVA. A significant F says only that the three means are not all equal. It does not say the focal mean is the highest. That is why the table also reports two one-sided paired contrasts of the focal definition against each orbiting definition. An item is marked Retain only when F and both contrasts are significant.

Colquitt et al.’s evaluation criteria

Colquitt, Sabey, Rodell, and Hill (2019) separated two questions that earlier work blended together. Definitional correspondence asks whether a scale’s items match the focal definition (psa, HTC). Definitional distinctiveness asks whether they match it better than they match orbiting definitions (csv, HTD).

They ran both designs on 112 published scales with naive judges drawn from the kind of population the scale would be used with, averaged each index across a scale’s items, and set the bands at percentiles of that distribution. A scale is Very strong when its mean sits at the 80th percentile or higher among those scales, and so on down. Because closely related constructs are harder to separate, they also gave norms conditional on how strongly the focal scale correlates with its orbiting scales.

Which design to use

The sort is fast for judges and its indices are easy to explain, but it discards information: a judge who sees an item as a near-tie between two definitions still casts one vote. The rating design keeps that information and supports significance tests on every contrast, at the price of three ratings per item per judge.

Pick orbiting constructs that are genuinely close to the focal one. Distant orbits make every scale look distinctive, and that is exactly why Colquitt et al. supplied correlation-conditional norms.
